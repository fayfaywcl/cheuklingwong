The Family Photo

A playful race to get everyone into frame before the countdown reaches zero.

Timeline

Technologies

Project Overview

A multiplayer party-game concept built around the familiar scramble of taking a family photo. Players race through a chaotic space and try to reach the camera zone before the timer ends, then see whether they made it into the shot.

View Prototype Result

Turning an everyday scramble into a game

I wanted to turn a normal life experience into a playful, competitive system: when a family photo is being taken, people rush to get ready, someone gets covered, someone misses the shot, and somebody may not be looking at the camera. I translated that small moment of chaos into a timed race with a clear goal.

Players can move around the area before the photo, but they cannot enter the camera zone until the countdown begins. When the timer reaches zero, the game captures the scene and checks whether the player is inside the photo area. For a more advanced version, the camera could ask for a particular pose or visible body part, making positioning as important as arriving on time.

References and design direction

Take Photo inspired me to think about camera capture and matching what appears in a frame. I also looked at Totally Secure Airport for the idea of checking a scene from a camera-like view. Stumble Guys helped me consider how obstacles and a shared race could make the approach to the photo zone more energetic.

The initial idea included a short 20-second countdown, obstacles such as walls and swinging hazards, and optional character accessories. I kept those as possible extensions. For this prototype, I focused on testing the movement, countdown, photo-zone check, and captured result.

Prototype testing

I started from the 3D objects and movement experiments I had made earlier, using a UFO as a temporary player character. The main test was whether a first-person player could move to a marked photo zone, whether a countdown could trigger capture, and whether the result could be shown clearly. Basic obstacles were secondary tests; accessories and a larger multiplayer count were future possibilities.

What worked

The core round loop works: a 10-second countdown gives the player time to reach the photo zone, then the game captures an image, checks whether the player is inside the zone, freezes movement, and displays the result UI. I added a restart flow using R and a world-space UI that follows the player. A dedicated result camera shows the captured image with result and restart text.

Debugging camera visibility and trigger detection helped me confirm each stage of the flow. The working capture and reset loop gave me a practical base for later testing of more players, obstacles, and pose requirements.
