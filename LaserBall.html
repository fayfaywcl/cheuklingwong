Bouncing Ball and Scrolling Lasers

A paddle-and-ball challenge where the player keeps a bouncing ball moving through repeating laser tags while balancing size, safety, and risk.

Timeline

2 weeks

Technologies

Unity, C#

Project Overview

An arcade prototype about keeping a bouncing ball in play through scrolling laser hazards, with ball size affecting both power and difficulty.

Watch Gameplay Demo

Finding a manageable mechanic

While searching IO games for inspiration, I was drawn to their repeated shapes, duplicated objects, and minimal mechanics. A laser-tag advertisement in my YouTube recommendations gave me a direction, and the way circles scale smoothly in Apple Watch UI stayed in my mind. I combined those references into a small prototype: control a bouncing ball and pass through scrolling laser tags without losing it.

First rule sketch

My first version of the rules was simple: the ball grows each time it passes a laser, a laser hit reduces its size, touching the bottom space three times ends the game, and each laser passed adds one point. I also considered different laser penalties, such as green reducing one level and red reducing two. I sketched the scene, ball-size levels, and several laser designs to see how the rules might read visually.

Bounding growth and making the start fair

I noticed that a ball that kept growing after every successful laser would eventually become unbalanced and look strange. I changed the open-ended growth into five fixed size levels:

Level 1
0.7×Level 2
1.0×Level 3
1.15×Level 4
1.3×Level 5
1.45×

During testing with friends, a bigger ball felt stronger and more rewarding, but it also became harder to dodge the lasers. That risk–reward effect emerged from testing rather than from my original plan.

I also caught an unfair opening rule: starting at Level 1 meant a single laser hit could end the game. I moved the starting level to Level 2. A laser hit now reduces the level; missing the paddle returns the ball to the same position and level while increasing the drop count. Three drops ends the game, as does reducing the ball to Level 0. This gives players room to recover from a drop while keeping laser hits consequential.

What I wanted the mid prototype to answer

I set out to test whether I could implement vertical scrolling lasers, control dynamic ball sizes with an array, and use a Singleton for score, drop count, scene switching, and sound. I also wanted to find out whether players could manage the paddle and bouncing ball together.

The stop-motion video prototype focuses on the key interactions: the ball bounces, can fall from the paddle, changes size when it hits a laser, and meets repeating laser colliders. The level and drop-count UI communicates the state, and a Game Over scene closes the loop.

Testing changed both the balance and the rules: capped growth, a Level 2 start, and separate consequences for laser hits and missed catches.

What Was Successful

This week, several systems worked successfully, and I am genuinely happy about them.

1. Rotating Laser System

I created a RotatingLaser script for my Laser Container prefab. With AI assistance, I reorganized the script so I could control its behavior more clearly in Unity’s Inspector. I can now choose between continuous rotation through a full circle and PingPong rotation between minimum and maximum angles. Adjusting the speed and angles in the Inspector made level design easier: I could shape each variation through the interface instead of rewriting the logic.

2. Hole Level Detection

I added a HoleLevelGate script so each hole can check the ball’s level. For example, if a hole is Level 3, a Level 2 ball loses a life while a Level 4 ball passes without an effect. This connects the ball-size system to the level design and gives ball level a purpose beyond appearance.

3. Safe Zone

I implemented a safe zone with a trigger block connected to the scrolling system. If the player moves correctly, they can reach a brief moment of safety.

Safe zone implementation

4. Heart Life System

Instead of showing lives as numbers, I switched to heart icons in the Canvas UI. An array of images switches between full and empty heart sprites based on the current life count. Seeing the hearts update made the interface feel more complete, and I was excited when the system worked.
