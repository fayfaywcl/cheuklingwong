Project Overview

A first-person narrative game where players help a fallen soldier make sense of fragmented memories through dialogue, journals, and exploration.

View Completed Game Flow

A story about memory and recovery

I imagined a dialogue-led experience in which a dead soldier tries to express what he thinks and feels, with confusion caused by forgotten or missing memories. After the player lures him to the therapy room, the player enters his mind. The soldier is no longer visible in this space: the empty memory environment becomes a metaphor for being inside his thoughts.

After several lines of dialogue, the soldier's war journal appears. Players can read entries for different days while his questions and answer choices remain available. The questions ask the player to connect the journal details and help the soldier understand what happened.

Making wrong answers part of the story

A correct choice leads to a response connected to that particular memory and advances to the next question. A wrong choice prompts the player to read again and try another answer. I chose not to add a separate punishment: the soldier's stress response already gives the moment emotional weight, and I did not want the interaction to feel like a score penalty.

During testing, using the same correct and incorrect follow-up text for every question felt too systematic. I rewrote the lines for each question so the feedback would sound like part of the soldier's dialogue and remain tied to the story. The current flow has three questions, with room to extend it, and ends with the soldier understanding what happened, resolving his regret, and becoming willing to leave.

Prototyping the interaction flow

I first drew the sequence from entering the therapy room to the final prompt, then made a video prototype to check how dialogue, journal browsing, answer choices, and feedback would fit together. I also drafted the soldier's entries to feel like a plausible personal war journal, with misunderstandings and missing details that could lead to regret.

The working flow now supports normal dialogue, journal-day buttons, question dialogue, question-specific correct and wrong responses, retrying, and a smooth ending. The journal buttons switch entries while the question stays available, so players can look for information without losing their place.

Improving player guidance

I first planned a guide panel before the journal. In the implementation, dialogue started as soon as the player entered the therapy room; clicking through the dialogue opened the journal immediately. That caused the automatic instruction panel to appear after the dialogue and overlap the journal, making the screen cluttered.

After trying to resolve the timing, I changed the guide into an optional Help button between the journal and the question. It opens a note-style instruction panel and toggles closed when clicked again. During implementation, I found that the button wiring and setup were causing the alignment and interaction problems. Simplifying the behavior to one toggle method made it easier to manage. I also increased the button and text sizes for readability.

I added a control guidance panel for onboarding and a lock during the journal sequence to control pacing. The completed flow showed that dialogue, the journal, choices, feedback, and the ending could work together while keeping the player oriented.

Building the memory atmosphere

I added ambient audio to the main scene to support its darker tone. I also made the blocks in the therapy room float up and down together, creating an unnatural, fuzzy feeling of entering a mind or memory. I considered moving the blocks randomly, but tests made that feel too chaotic and disorienting. Keeping them synchronized looked more controlled and intentional.

Game title and elevator pitch

I chose Echoes of the Fallen because “echoes” suggests lingering memories, while “fallen” refers both to the soldier and to the player entering his mind. My original pitch was:

Echoes of the Fallen is a first-person story game where players battle and heal broken soldiers by navigating their memories and confronting psychological trauma.

The intended audience is players interested in narrative-driven, meaningful gameplay. The project's planned formal elements bring together AI-driven dialogue, interactive journals, and 3D memory mazes. Its central mechanic reframes combat as a process of healing: players navigate difficult memories and reconstruct fragmented accounts instead of defeating enemies. The journal supports close reading and interpretation, while wrong answers lead to emotional feedback instead of punishment.

Photo capture as the closing moment

I designed a final photo interaction for the end of the therapy-room journey. After the questions and 3D pixel maze, the player reaches the end of a floating bridge and approaches a camera. A nearby prompt explains that pressing C activates it; left mouse click takes the photo. The camera frame appears only after the player approaches and activates the object, which makes the interaction feel like a deliberate action rather than a feature that is always on.

The implemented flow opens camera mode, captures the current view, shows it in the interface, plays a flash and capture sound, and overlays a soldier image on the final photo. The photograph develops for a moment, then a five-second “Returning…” countdown brings the player back to the same place in the mansion therapy room. I chose a countdown because I had already used timed scene transitions, and here it also gives the player a brief goodbye before returning.

While debugging, I found that clicking the shutter could close all the picture frames at once. I added Debug.Log messages through the trigger, camera pickup, camera opening, capture click, and preview display to trace the order of events. I also removed the earlier answer trigger zones: the photo sequence and countdown now handle the return to the mansion, so the player no longer needs to stand in one of several specific zones.

What Was Successful

Overall, the ideation and refinement process worked well in improving the game flow through better player guidance and control systems. I added a control guidance panel to help players understand the input system more clearly, which significantly improved onboarding and reduced confusion during gameplay. I also added a lock feature during the journal sequence to better control pacing and player interaction. This helped refine the overall game flow in the therapy room sequence, making the experience more structured and easier to follow.

Overall, these changes improved both usability and player comprehension which ensure that players can engage with the mechanics without needing external explanation.
